Architecture of stability: 15 years of pollinator sharing reveal a role of stable core species

Jakub Štenc1,2,3, Sailee P. Sakhalkar4, Jiří Hadrava2, Michael Mikát2, Eva Matoušková1, Helena Pijálková2, Tadeáš Ryšan2,5, Petr Švanda1,2, Martin Freudenfeld1, Eva Horčičková6, Zdeněk Janovský7, Jakub Straka2, Jordi Bosch3

1Charles University (Botany) | 2Charles University (Zoology) | 3CREAF, Spain

Atmospheric introduction to the Handrkov meadow: Species-rich submontane grassland ecosystem where dozens of entomophilous plants bloom simultaneously, all sharing a finite pool of flower visitors.

Interactions

Start with pollination biology: In species-rich meadows, pollinators are a shared, limiting mutualistic service. Co-flowering plants do not interact in isolation; sharing insect visitors mediates indirect interactions—both facilitation (magnet plants) and competition (heterospecific pollen deposition, stigma clogging, pollen dilution). The paradox: Short-term network studies show massive year-to-year turnover and rewiring. Yet, diverse plant communities persist for decades. Is this sharing web in constant chaotic drift, or does an underlying stable architecture anchor the community?

Step 1 (First visit links): We start from empirical flower visits in the field—an insect landing on a flower. Step 2 (Bipartite Web): As dozens of co-flowering plant species and hundreds of insect visitors interact across the meadow, these individual visits weave together into a bipartite plant-pollinator interaction network. Step 3 (Shared Pollinator): Plants do not interact directly; they interact through shared flower visitors. Here, Eristalis visits both Achillea and Succisa (yellow lines). Step 4 (Living Bridge / Indirect Pollen Link): Because the shared pollinator moves between both flowers, an indirect functional link connects the two plant species (blue line). Step 5 (Pollinator-Sharing Network): By projecting these shared visits across the whole community, we reveal the unipartite pollinator-sharing web—a topological network of plants anchored by dense shared connections!

Slide: What actually makes a species part of the core? Network topology defines this using k-core decomposition (Seidman 1983). [Click 1 – onion]: How do we decide which species form the core? Think of the network as an onion: we peel it layer by layer, and what is left at the centre is the core. Think of it like peeling an onion: - Step 1 (The Entire Web): We start with all species connected (k >= 1). Outer peripheral species (coral/red) connect by only a single link. - Step 2 (Iterative Peeling): We peel away outer shells: any species whose degree drops below k is successively pruned. Single-link leaves fall away first. - Step 3 (The Invariant Core): At k_max, only the mutually reinforcing central backbone remains (navy hubs with gold centers). These core hubs don’t just have visits—they link to each other, creating the structural foundation that holds the entire community together. - Step 5 (Every year): We repeat this for each of the 15 annual networks. Filled dot = species is central that year (standardized coreness Gc > 1). Centaurea jacea (core) is central in all 15 years; Daucus carota (the next-ranked, peripheral species, present all 15 years) only in 5 (2012–14, 2022–23). Centrality ranks are strongly preserved between years (Kendall’s W = 0.64 vs 0.23 expected by chance, p = 0.001; consecutive years Spearman median 0.77). Centaurea jacea sits above the null in all 15 years.

Robustness

Meadow pollinator sharing network under simulated extinction scenarios

Co-extinction simulation (Memmott et al. 2004) on Handrkov pollination web

Slide 6: Here is how network theory formalizes robustness (Memmott et al. 2004). Step 0: Title only. Step 0b (broken network icon): What happens to the network when species disappear? Step 1 (Axes & Intact Web): Here are the co-extinction axes: proportion of plants extinct vs pollinators surviving, alongside our intact Handrkov sharing network. Step 2 (Random Loss): When species disappear at random, robustness shows a steady, intermediate decline. Step 3 (Peripheral Spoke Loss): When peripheral species are removed first (red dashed line), the curve stays flat near 100%! Almost all pollinators survive because the central core anchors them. High robustness (R ≈ 0.85). Step 4 (Core Hub Collapse): BUT when core hubs collapse (solid navy line), the curve plummets immediately! Losing Centaurea and Succisa causes massive secondary cascades. Low robustness (R ≈ 0.45). Bridge (spoken, no extra click): But this is one snapshot. How does this picture change across 15 years of increasingly dry summers?

Drought

Data: TerraClimate (Abatzoglou et al., 2018), Handrkov locality (1975–2025)

In theory, networks collapse when the core is removed. But in reality, networks change in time as they are under pressure from the environment—specifically drought. Looking across the last 50 years at our meadow, drought has become an increasingly severe and persistent pressure.

Drought

Data: TerraClimate (Abatzoglou et al., 2018), Handrkov locality (1975–2025)

And especially in the last 15 years—the exact window of our field survey—drought seems to be a reoccurring pressure on the system.

Drought

Data: TerraClimate (Abatzoglou et al., 2018), Handrkov locality (2011–2025)

How does reoccurring drought affect network robustness?

Zooming into our 15-year period: only 2013 and 2021 were wet summers. 2018 was the driest, followed by 2015, 2019 and 2025, and 2023 was also dry. Drought is not a single event here but a recurring condition. So our key question is: How does this reoccurring drought affect network robustness?

Robustness over Time

Same 6 core species, but losing them costs more in dry years and more every year

We simulated secondary extinction cascades (Memmott et al. 2004). Robustness R is the area under the co-extinction curve. Orange bars at the bottom = summer drought severity. Periphery removed first: robustness stays high every year (R = 0.81–0.91). Random order: intermediate (R = 0.65–0.75). Core removed first: always the most fragile, and getting worse. R = 0.69 in 2011, dips in the dry years 2015 and 2018 (≈ 0.45), recovers in 2020–2022 (≈ 0.55), and reaches its lowest values in 2023–2025 (0.41–0.43). So the dependence on the core is driven by drought AND by a long-term trend: core-removal R declines with drier summers (p = 0.005) and with year (p = 0.001). The gap ΔR = R_periph − R_core grows from 0.15 (2011) to 0.50 (2025). [Advance – circularity overlay; ΔR +0.022/yr, p = 0.014]: Someone may say: of course removing hubs hurts more. But the core identity is fixed for all 15 years. A fixed definition cannot by itself produce a gap that changes with weather and grows over time.

Why?

The core holds 14% of flowers but gets 67% of visits

Slide: Why does losing the core cost more and more? Step 1 (navy): share of all pollinator visits that go to the 6 core species. It rises from 34–55% (2011–14) to 84–88% (2023–25), +2.3 percentage points per year (GLS-AR1, p = 0.001), and is higher in drier summers (p = 0.004). Peaks in 2015 and 2018. Step 2 (grey): share of flowers produced by the core species. Always far below the visit line: on average 14% of flowers vs 67% of visits over 15 years. [Advance – overlay]: Pollinators use the core far beyond its flower supply. The more visits concentrate on the core, the more damage its loss does: that is why the robustness gap grows. Caveat if asked: core flowering also increased in recent years (up to 31% in 2025), so part of the rise in visits follows more core flowers. In dry years peripheral flowering drops (~3× along the drought gradient, p = 0.032; backup slide) while core flowering does not.

Take-Home Messages

01 Persistent Core

6 species stay central for 15 years

02 Rising Dependence

Drought shifts pollinators onto the core

03 Protect the Core

Should we protect species that stay central and keep flowering through perturbations?
  1. Architecture: The 15-year dataset reveals that network coreness is topologically invariant and identity-based (LMM p = 1.8e-73, explaining 38% of centrality variance after controlling for flower abundance).
  2. Rising Core Dependence (figure: 46% → 86% = mean core share of visits 2011–14 vs 2023–25): Peripheral flowering declines ~3-fold along the drought gradient (p = 0.032), core flowering does not (p = 0.79). The share of visits going to the core rose from 34–55% to 84–88%, and the robustness gap between losing the core and losing the periphery grew from 0.15 to 0.50 over 15 years.
  3. Conservation: Grassland management should look beyond simple species counts and protect the species that combine a persistent hub role with drought-stable flowering (e.g. Centaurea, Succisa, Selinum).

Appendix & Backup Slides

Q&A: Is It Circular?

The Circularity Skeptic: “Core species are high-degree hubs by definition. Doesn’t removing them automatically reduce robustness mathematically, making core importance a tautology?”

Pillar 1
Climatic Decoupling

If circular, core removal would cost the same every year.

Instead, core-loss robustness ranges from 0.69 to 0.41 and drops in drier summers (\(p = 0.005\)).

Weather cannot change a mathematical tautology.

Pillar 2
Static vs. Dynamic Gap

Core identity is static (the same 6 species across 15 yrs from null models).

Yet the fragility gap (\(\Delta R\)) is dynamic: it surges with summer drought (\(p = 0.018\)) and intensifies over 15 years (\(p = 0.014\)).

A static definition cannot generate a 15-year temporal trend.

Pillar 3
Empirical Biology

Driven by plant physiology, not network geometry:

Peripheral flowering drops ~3× along the drought gradient (\(\beta = -28.0, p = 0.032\)), while core flowering does not.

Pollinators concentrate onto the core (34–55% → 84–88% of visits), so losing it removes most of the remaining forage.

Q&A Defense against circularity: 1. Environmental state-dependence: If core importance were a mathematical circularity of high degree, core loss would cost the same every year. Instead core-removal R varies from 0.69 (2011) to 0.41 (2025) and is lower in drier summers (p = 0.005). 2. Temporal decoupling: Core identity is static across 15 years, but the robustness gap Delta R surges with drought severity (p = 0.018) and increases over time (+0.022/yr, p = 0.014). 3. Biological mechanism: Peripheral floral abundance drops ~3-fold along the drought gradient while core flowering is stable, so pollinators concentrate on the core (share of visits 34–55% in 2011–14 vs 84–88% in 2023–25). Honest caveat: core removal is ALWAYS worse than peripheral removal, partly by construction. The argument is about the size of the gap changing, not about its sign.

Appendix: Floral Response to Drought

The Life-Raft Mechanism: Peripheral flowering drops ~3× across the drought gradient (\(\beta = -28.0, p = 0.032\)), core flowering shows no detectable drop (\(\beta = +0.9, p = 0.79\)) \(\rightarrow\) pollinators concentrate on the core: from 34–55% of visits (2011–14) to 84–88% (2023–25).

Backup: floral response of core vs peripheral species to summer drought. Floral asymmetry: across the drought gradient, the fitted peripheral floral abundance falls about 3-fold (from ~140 to ~46), while the 6 core species keep a steady display. In the two driest years (2015, 2018) peripheral flowering was ~10× below a typical year, but 2019 and 2025 were also dry and peripheral flowering held up much better, so the fitted ~3× is the fair summary. Note: the core species are not the most abundant flowers; peripheral species together have ~6× more flowers. The core is central despite that. Consequence: the share of all pollinator visits going to the core rose from 34–55% (2011–14) to 84–88% (2023–25).

Appendix: Core Dependence (\(\Delta R\))

Core dependence (\(\Delta R = R_{\text{periph}} - R_{\text{core}}\)) surges during acute summer droughts (\(p = 0.018\)) and steadily intensifies over time (\(+0.022/\text{yr}, p = 0.014\)).

Backup Slide: Core dependence surges during droughts and steadily rises across 15 years. When severe droughts strike, losing the core causes far more catastrophic secondary cascades than losing peripheral species.

Appendix: Species Roles

Multi-year Guimerà & Amaral modularity cartography: core species consistently occupy high within-module degree (z = 0.86-2.39). Longitudinal Markov role transitions demonstrate hub fidelity: core species retain hub status 59.2% of years and have 70.4% rebound probability with <=4% dropout risk, vs 3.5% hub attainment for peripherals.

Appendix: Abundance vs Degree Correlation

Correlation between floral abundance and species degree fluctuates across years, dropping significantly during droughts (e.g. 2011 and 2018), proving that abundance does not dictate network roles.

Appendix: Pollinator Concentration

SI Appendix Fig S7: Direct empirical proof of pollinator dietary restriction during droughts. When peripheral plants wither, pollinators dynamically re-allocate foraging to the core, receiving +62% to +66% excess visits beyond abundance expectations.
