Atmospheric introduction to the Handrkov meadow: Species-rich submontane grassland ecosystem where dozens of entomophilous plants bloom simultaneously, all sharing a finite pool of flower visitors.

The Paradox of Pollinator Sharing

Start with pollination biology: In species-rich meadows, pollinators are a shared, limiting mutualistic service. Co-flowering plants do not interact in isolation; sharing insect visitors mediates indirect interactions—both facilitation (magnet plants) and competition (heterospecific pollen deposition, stigma clogging, pollen dilution). The paradox: Short-term network studies show massive year-to-year turnover and rewiring. Yet, diverse plant communities persist for decades. Is this sharing web in constant chaotic drift, or does an underlying stable architecture anchor the community?

🌐 Core Hub Failure

Frankfurt & Heathrow / Centaurea & Succisa

  • If Frankfurt or Heathrow shuts down, the entire continental system collapses
  • If the 6 Core plant hubs fail, shared pollinator services unravel across the meadow
  • Critical question: How robust is this network when extreme drought hits?

The Ecological Dilemma: Across 15 years, does climate stress break the core, or does the core buffer the community?

This sets up the core tension of the talk: Network theory teaches us that scale-free / hub-and-spoke networks are remarkably robust to random failure of spokes, but catastrophically fragile to targeted attack on hubs. In Handrkov, during 15 years, we experienced unprecedented summer droughts (2015, 2018). What happened to the core? What happened to the whole community? Let’s look at the climate history of the meadow!

Macro-Dynamics: Two Decoupled Timescales

Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) of annual directed PAC matrices (Bray-Curtis, stress = 0.106). Vector fitting identifies two decoupled, orthogonal axes: 1. Axis 1 (Horizontal - Acute Climate Pulse): Summer drought severity (r² = 0.55, p = 0.008) and summer temperature (r² = 0.67, p = 0.001) displace the network during severe droughts (2015 and 2018) far to the right into centralized topological states. 2. Axis 2 (Vertical - Decadal Press): Steady successional progression across 15 years (r² = 0.71, p = 0.0008). Crucially: Notice the hysteresis! When rainfall returned in 2020-2021, the network shifted back horizontally along Axis 1, but NEVER returned to its pre-2015 baseline along Axis 2.

Identifying the Meadow’s Permanent Hubs

Are the network hubs just an ephemeral accident of whoever happens to produce the most flowers in a given year?

Or does the meadow possess a permanent set of topological anchors that maintain core roles across 15 years—irrespective of climatic extremes?

Recall our airport analogy: is Frankfurt an international hub because it randomly had an extra runway open on a sunny Tuesday? Or because of permanent structural routing? In pollination networks, the central question is whether network hubs are merely abundant generalists, or whether specific plant identities provide permanent community cohesion. To test this, we compared every species against a presence-conditional null model across all 15 years.

Discovering the True Core

Are network hubs just random flowers that happen to bloom heavily? We tested each species’ 15-year standardized coreness against a conservative, presence-conditional (flowering-only) null model (1,000 perms/yr). Exactly 6 species maintained statistically significant excess centrality: Centaurea jacea, Selinum carvifolia, Succisa pratensis, Pimpinella saxifraga, Achillea ptarmica, and Ranunculus acris (all P_adj <= 0.0015).

Plant Identity Matters: Not an Abundance Lottery!

The Neutral Hypothesis

  • Hubs are just whichever plants produce the most flowers.
  • Coreness drifts randomly with plant density.
  • Species traits play no role.

Linear Mixed Model (Centrality)

  • Plant Identity: explains 38% of variance
  • Floral Abundance: explains 44% of variance
  • LRT for Plant Identity:
    \(\chi^2 = 328.8, \mathbf{p = 1.8 \times 10^{-73}}\)


Pollinators actively seek out core species beyond their sheer floral abundance.
The core is an enduring architectural feature grounded in intrinsic floral syndromes and attractiveness.

Even after controlling for raw flower count, species identity explains nearly 40% of the variance. Topological invariance: In multi-year modularity cartography (Guimerà & Amaral), these exact same 6 species emerge as permanent module hubs (z = 0.86-2.39). Markov role transitions show core species have a 59.2% probability of remaining hubs year-to-year and a 70.4% rebound probability, compared to only 3.5% for peripherals.

Secondary Extinctions: The Fragility Flip

During normal years: The meadow exhibits high structural redundancy (\(R \approx 0.63\text{--}0.84\)).
During severe droughts (2015, 2018): Core loss triggers catastrophic collapse (\(R \approx 0.45\)), while peripheral loss is easily buffered (\(R \approx 0.83\)). The Robustness Gap (\(\Delta R\)) surges!

We simulated secondary extinction cascades (Memmott et al. 2004). Robustness R is the area under the co-extinction curve. In non-drought years, pollinators easily switch partners. But in 2015 and 2018, the network’s survival becomes almost exclusively dependent on the 6 core species.

What Causes the Flip? A 3-Step Mechanistic Cascade

1. Vulnerability (\(\Delta R\))

Core dependence surges during summer droughts (\(p = 0.018\)) and rises over time (\(+0.022/\text{yr}\)).

2. Floral Asymmetry

Periphery crashes 10-fold (\(\beta = -28.0, p = 0.032\)).
Core is completely drought-buffered (\(\beta = +0.9, p = 0.792\)).

3. Motif Scaffolding

Core motifs surge to 95–99% in drought years, predicting \(84\%\) of network fragility (\(R^2 = 0.84\)).


The Life-Raft Mechanism: Peripheral flowers dry up \(\rightarrow\) pollinators concentrate exclusively on drought-buffered core heads (+66% excess visits) \(\rightarrow\) the core becomes the community’s sole life-support system.

Take-Home Messages for Pollination Ecology

  1. Networks are dynamic but anchored: High annual rewiring occurs around a persistent core of 6 plant species that stabilizes the community.
  2. Plant Identity drives centrality: The core is not a neutral abundance lottery; species identity independently explains 38% of network centrality (\(p = 1.8 \times 10^{-73}\)).
  3. Decoupled timescales: Acute drought shocks (Axis 1) trigger immediate centralization, while decadal succession (Axis 2) leaves lasting hysteresis.
  4. The Life-Raft Mechanism: Severe drought collapses peripheral floral displays 10-fold, forcing pollinators to crowd onto drought-buffered core hubs (95–99% core-anchored motifs).
  5. Targeted Conservation: Preserving pollination networks against climate extremes requires safeguarding keystone plants that unite high floral attractiveness with deep physiological drought tolerance.
